Welcome to the Daily State of AI for Thursday, November 26th, 2026.

On Tuesday night, computer scientist Scott Aaronson had friends and colleagues over for dinner. Instead of relaxing between courses, the table ended up huddled around phones and a laptop, refreshing GitHub.

That is how major mathematical breakthroughs arrive now. Frontier labs have stopped waiting for press releases, blog posts, or even tweets. They just push papers straight to code repositories.

Yet by Wednesday afternoon, if you opened X, you would barely know anyone had proven anything in pure math. Viral posts with thousands of likes were warning that frontier labs had secretly cracked RSA encryption and could soon forge software updates across the internet.

Those two scenes look like separate worlds: quiet math commits on GitHub, and a cybersecurity scare on social media. They are actually two halves of the same shift. AI has just turned frontier mathematics into a dual-use technology, splitting the field right down the middle.

Look at the pace of what landed on GitHub in forty-eight hours. While Aaronson's dinner guests were still absorbing last week's Alpöge and Anthropic proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, OpenAI uploaded a claim pinning down the exact exponent of matrix multiplication at nine-fourths. That includes a lower bound above two, a barrier Aaronson once thought was nearly as hard as P versus NP. By dessert, OpenAI added matching upper and lower bounds on the number of linear gates needed for the Fourier transform, beating the classic 1960s Fast Fourier Transform.

Just as striking as the answers is how OpenAI reached them. A single new framework built on tensor geometry powered multiple major results at once, undercutting the comforting idea that AI only stitches together one-off tricks. Aaronson wrote that he now assumes proofs coming directly from OpenAI or Anthropic are correct unless shown otherwise.

Then Wednesday morning, Google Research escalated its own quiet GitHub repository. After posting four AI-generated papers over the previous month, Google committed six more at once with no announcement. Google says its Cogentic agent harness, running on Gemini 4, produced all six with no human interaction after the initial prompt, tackling problems from Harary's 1964 graph reconstruction conjecture and the log-rank conjecture to two new results on integer factoring.

Those factoring papers immediately fueled the online panic, so it helps to separate public math from private rumor. Google's two factoring papers do not threaten RSA. One gives an unconditional algorithm without unproven number-theoretic assumptions; the other achieves deterministic subexponential factoring without randomness. After checking the repository, Aaronson confirmed both are major theoretical results with no practical impact on cryptography. Outside mathematicians can read the PDFs today, though Google has not yet released the companion Lean 4 formalizations it says are complete.

The real alarm came from a brief paragraph in Aaronson's Wednesday post. He wrote that he is virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources, that frontier labs are sitting on one or more unreleased cryptographic bombshells. Notice what Aaronson did not say. He deliberately refused to name RSA or claim anyone had factored large numbers in polynomial time, warning that technical specifics were likely getting mangled in a game of telephone. Popular AI commentators and aggregator accounts on X filled in those blanks themselves, turning a calibrated warning into viral claims that RSA was dead.

Still, the math deluge and the cryptography rumors collided this week for a deeper reason. In theoretical computer science, lower bounds and algorithms live in the same house. When AI builds powerful new mathematical machinery, it does not only prove reassuring limits on what computers cannot do. It also uncovers unexpected algorithms for what they can, and some of those algorithms will hit deployed cryptosystems.

That is why the outside view feels so disorienting right now. Labs quietly commit safe theorems to GitHub at dinner-table speed, with zero fanfare. Anything touching live security stays behind closed doors, leaving everyone else swinging between shrugging off abstract math and panicking over half-heard rumors.

Aaronson wrote that this past week feels like the opening chapter of an AI singularity, hoping we get lucky and it stays confined to hard math. For today, he offered simpler advice: spend Thanksgiving with the people you love.
