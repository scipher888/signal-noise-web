Embedded assessors get deep access, no findings.

November 17, 2026

Audio on Spotify

AI-generated · may contain errors · Editor's notes

Three questions are driving discussion today among people building and studying these systems. How independent can safety assessments be when the assessors are embedded inside a company training run. What counts as evidence that artificial intelligence is making scientists more productive. And whether teams should keep building sprawling multi-agent systems or fold the work back into a single agent.

The first question comes from OpenAI and its first phase of embedded assessments. This is company-reported, and no assessor findings have been published yet, so the debate is about access and structure rather than results.

OpenAI says it has signed agreements with four outside groups, Apollo Research, METR, Transluce, and Goodfire. OpenAI presents this as independent scrutiny of how it identifies and manages risks. The access described is deep. Depending on what each evaluation needs, assessors can use some of the same internal resources OpenAI researchers use, including model checkpoints, source code, training and evaluation data, monitoring systems, technical documentation, and discussions with the researchers and engineers responsible for the work.

The specific assignments are narrow and concrete. METR will assess misaligned behavior in OpenAI's largest reinforcement learning run to date, including failures that existing tests and monitors may have missed. Apollo Research will assess whether models could covertly work against their developers' intentions and whether safeguards would detect such behavior. Those are controlled investigations inside training, not tests of autonomous behavior in ordinary use.

The independence question turns on two limits OpenAI itself states. Assessors maintain independence in publishing their assessments, but OpenAI will retain the right to redact confidential information, consistent with previous third-party engagements. And Apollo Research and Goodfire accept financial compensation for their work, although OpenAI says compensation is not contingent on the results. Practitioners must judge how much scrutiny survives redaction and paid embedded work. At this stage, there are no findings to weigh.

The second question is what OpenAI's study of scientists actually measured. OpenAI frames the result as scientists nearly doubling productivity, but the company-reported details are more limited, and the study does not establish causal effects.

OpenAI's Economic Research team studied more than 580,000 scientific messages from 1,819 scientists in the ChatGPT for Academic Researchers summer pilot, and surveyed 251 participants. The 1.9 figure rests on those surveyed participants' own estimate that the same work, at the same quality, would have taken 1.9 times as long without AI. Productivity here is self-reported, and the discovery results are expectations, not observed discoveries. The study has not been independently replicated.

That distinction matters because this is assisted performance, not autonomous performance. Scientists were using ChatGPT across idea development, data analysis, and writing code. The measure is how long participants felt the same work would have taken without the tool, not a controlled comparison of output quality, error rates, or verified new findings, and not a test of an agent working on its own.

The third question is whether a single agent with skills and tools is really beating multi-agent setups. Ethan Mollick, an independent academic commentator at Wharton, shared Anthropic's guidance and said it seems right and amounts to a condemnation of many agentic solutions and harnesses being built at companies. That is Mollick's interpretation, not a measurement, and it is the view of one commentator, not a consensus.

The underlying source is an Anthropic best-practices article dated October 22, 2026, newly circulating today. It is company-reported and based on Anthropic's own comparisons across several unnamed enterprise deployments, not independently replicated. Anthropic says that in those comparisons, a single agent with skills frequently outperformed both the one-prompt-for-everything design and the subagent design on quality, and often at lower cost and latency per task. Anthropic cites one customer that evaluated its multi-agent harness against a single agent loop and saw roughly 2 times faster runtime with the single agent. Anthropic also says it reduced Claude Code's system prompt for more advanced models by over 80 percent, including cuts related to progressively disclosing skills and tools.

Anthropic is careful to add that subagents still have their place, including at the research frontier. Its practical advice is to build a small end-to-end evaluation, fold in one subagent, and keep it as a subagent only if the folded version scores worse, then repeat. For practitioners, the question is whether their own evaluations in real-world deployments show the same pattern Anthropic reports in selected cases.
