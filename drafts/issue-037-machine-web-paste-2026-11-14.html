Where the Starship Catch Ranks, and What It Could Change

Flight 15 comes sixth among history's hardest engineering feats, and its effects could reach people who never leave the ground.

Judged purely by difficulty, with every project in history allowed modern tools, today's catch of Starship Flight 15 ranks sixth among engineering achievements, and it has a fair chance of climbing. One caveat first: I can't independently confirm the flight, so I am taking it as described and assuming the upper stage returned from orbital speed and was caught by its launch tower. Boosters have been caught since October 2024, and the ship was the missing half.

The modern-tools rule reshapes the list. With 2026 computers, materials and factories, the pyramids and the Panama Canal become large but routine contracts. Even the Manhattan Project shrinks, because its central problems have since been solved many times over. What stays hard is whatever is still hard today: work near physical limits, with thousands of parts that must all perform and few chances to fix a mistake. Any such ranking is a judgment call, so here is the reasoning.

First is the leading-edge chip factory and the extreme-ultraviolet printing machines inside it. To make the light, a laser blasts 50,000 droplets of molten tin every second. The light bounces off mirrors so smooth that, if one were enlarged to the size of Germany, its largest bump would stand no more than about a millimeter high. It prints circuit patterns onto silicon layer upon layer, each aligned to the last within a few atoms' width. The product is tens of billions of working transistors on a chip the size of a fingernail, turned out by the million. The rule gives this entry no discount, since these machines are the modern tools.

Second is Apollo. Better computers do not shorten the 384,000 kilometers to the Moon or soften the consequences of a failed engine with a crew aboard. Landing people there and bringing them home takes a long chain of maneuvers in which every link must hold, and as far as I know nobody has done it since 1972. Money and politics explain part of that gap, though not all of it.

Third is the James Webb Space Telescope, which unfolded itself on the way to a post 1.5 million kilometers from Earth, beyond the reach of any repair crew, through a sequence with 344 points where a single failure could have ended the mission. Fourth is the Large Hadron Collider, a 27-kilometer ring of superconducting magnets kept colder than deep space, steering proton beams that each carry the energy of a speeding train into collisions narrower than a hair. Fifth is LIGO, which detects ripples in space from colliding black holes by sensing a change in the length of its four-kilometer arms of about one ten-thousandth the width of a proton.

Starship's catch comes sixth, ahead of the International Space Station, which a ship this size would have made far simpler to build. For sixty years many engineers doubted that a fully reusable orbital rocket could carry a useful load, because the weight of heat shields and landing fuel comes straight out of the cargo. Getting past that took the most powerful flying machine ever built, with more than twice the thrust of the Saturn V, engines that run at higher pressures than any flown before, and a 50-meter ship that survives reentry at about 27,000 kilometers per hour, swings upright and settles onto two steel arms.

It sits below the top five for two reasons. Its builders could learn by destroying uncrewed prototypes, which is a sound method but a freedom Apollo and Webb never had. And every entry above it has done its full job, most of them for years, while a single catch is not yet reuse. The Space Shuttle also came home, then needed months of costly repair before each flight. If this ship flies again within days, I would move it up.

What follows from the catch matters more than its rank. Until now every orbital rocket threw away some or all of itself on each flight, as if an airline scrapped the plane after every trip. The Shuttle cost roughly $50,000 per kilogram delivered to orbit, and today's partly reusable rockets charge a few thousand. A fully reusable ship that flies often could bring that to a few hundred or less. At that price the question changes from what can justify a launch to what is worth doing in space.

Take the optimistic but plausible case. For decades most people will feel the change as better services on the ground, and only later as a chance to go. In ten years phones connect straight to satellites, so dead zones disappear and most of the roughly two billion people still offline come within reach of a signal. Swarms of Earth-watching satellites spot wildfires within minutes, trace methane leaks to single wells and sharpen weather forecasts. Space telescopes get cheaper because they no longer need to fold up as Webb did and can be built heavy and simple. Crews live on the Moon for weeks at a time.

In twenty-five years the Moon has a permanently staffed base, run much like an Antarctic research station, and the first crews have walked on Mars. A seat to orbit costs about what a luxury car does. You probably haven't gone, but you know someone who has. Telescopes far larger than Webb examine the air of Earth-sized planets for signs of life, so a child born today could see the first real evidence on whether we are alone. A few products, such as certain drugs and specialty optical fiber, are made in orbit. Whether solar power or computing in orbit pays its way is still unsettled.

In fifty years several thousand people live off Earth and space has become a workplace. Technicians and nurses sign up for tours on the Moon the way people now take jobs on offshore oil rigs, and fuel and metal are produced there for use in space. A trip to orbit is a once-in-a-lifetime purchase for a middle-class family, like an Antarctic cruise today. It never becomes bus fare, because reaching orbit burns many times the fuel per passenger of a long-haul flight.

In a hundred years the hopeful picture is hundreds of thousands of people living on the Moon, on Mars and in orbit, with one settlement close to being able to survive if the ships stopped coming. Moving there is a hard but real choice, as emigrating to Australia was in 1850. Some of the most energy-hungry industry has left the planet, and Earth is cleaner for it. Aviation offers a precedent: a century after the Wright brothers, airlines were carrying more than a billion passengers a year.

None of this is guaranteed. Concorde and the Shuttle both worked, and neither created the market its builders expected. The catch shows the physics works, while the economics still depend on turning a ship around in days and flying it hundreds of times. Crowded orbits, unwritten rules and the unknown effects of low gravity on the human body could each slow things down. Still, the main obstacle for sixty years was that the vehicle was thrown away after one use, and that obstacle has now been removed at least once.
