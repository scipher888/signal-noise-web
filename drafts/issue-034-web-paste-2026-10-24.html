We Are Arguing About the Speed of AI. We Have No Plan for the Crash.

One morning, your bank account doesn't open. Not because it's empty — because the bank cannot prove it ever existed. Your card declines at the grocery store. The register works, but the payment network behind it doesn't. Trucks are full, warehouses are stocked, but no one can verify orders, extend credit, or pay drivers. At the hospital down the street, the power flickers, the backup generators kick in, and then the electronic records system goes dark.

Nothing has been bombed. No army has crossed a border. A rogue actor — human, assisted by AI, or eventually AI acting with tools no single hacker could wield alone — has corrupted, encrypted or erased the digital layer modern life quietly depends on: banking records, communications, logistics, utilities. Food exists. Fuel exists. Doctors exist. The system that coordinates them does not.

This is the scenario missing from our AI debate. Washington, Silicon Valley and the commentariat are locked in a theological war between two camps: pause or accelerate. Slow down before we build something we cannot control, say the doomers. Sprint ahead of China and open-source imitators or lose the future, say the accelerationists. Both sides talk as if the only decision that matters is how fast we move.

It is the wrong question. Or at least, it is dangerously incomplete. The more urgent question is not whether we can prevent every AI-enabled catastrophe forever. It's what happens the day prevention fails. Right now, we have no credible answer.

Start with what is actually at stake. Civilization is not an abstraction. It is the reason a person who is homeless, addicted or mentally ill in Los Angeles today can still survive — not comfortably, not justly, but survive — in a way that would have been unimaginable for most of human history. It is the reason genius compounds. An Elon Musk born 20,000 years ago builds nothing recognizable. A brilliant mind without writing, metallurgy, electricity, capital markets and supply chains dies having sharpened a better stone. Modern civilization is the platform that turns individual talent into rockets, vaccines and supercomputers. Lose the platform, and talent hardly matters.

That platform is now digital, interdependent and astonishingly fragile. Money is not mostly cash in vaults; it is entries in databases. Hospitals do not run on paper charts; they run on networked records, scheduling and billing systems. Grocery stores do not reorder by intuition; automated logistics move food across continents on just-in-time schedules measured in hours. Power, water, communications, finance and transportation do not fail independently anymore. They fail together, because they depend on the same internet, the same cloud providers, the same software supply chains. We got a preview in July 2024, when one faulty software update, with no hacker and no AI involved, crashed an estimated 8.5 million Windows computers and grounded flights, delayed surgeries and knocked out some 911 systems.

AI does not invent this vulnerability. Hackers, ransomware gangs and hostile states already probe it every day. What AI does is change the economics of attack. It lowers the skill floor, raises the speed ceiling, and multiplies the number of plausible attackers. Systems that can write code, find vulnerabilities, craft flawless phishing campaigns in any language, and operate persistently without sleep turn what once required an elite team into a scalable process. Open-weight models ensure those capabilities will not stay locked inside a few responsible labs. Foreign adversaries will not pause because we do.

That is why both dominant strategies fail on their own terms. A pause, even if America could enforce one — and it cannot globally — buys time for alignment research that may help and may never be complete. In the meantime, less cautious actors keep building. Acceleration, meanwhile, rests on a heroic assumption: that we will always stay one step ahead, defensively, forever. One step ahead until capabilities plateau. One step ahead against every rogue employee, criminal network, terrorist cell and intelligence service on earth, in perpetuity. Defenders must be right every time; an attacker only has to be right once. That is not a strategy. That is a hope.

Aviation did not become safe by arguing only about whether planes should fly faster. It became safe by assuming crashes would happen and engineering for survivability: redundant systems, black boxes, emergency procedures, investigations that fed back into design. Nuclear power, finance after 2008, even seatbelts — every mature technology eventually learns the same lesson. Prevention is essential. Resilience is non-negotiable. With AI and critical infrastructure, we are still arguing about the accelerator while refusing to design the crash structure.

What would a crash plan actually look like? It starts with unfashionable, unglamorous work no one can fundraise off. Verified, offline, immutable backups of core financial records, tested not on paper but in live restoration exercises. A pre-agreed baseline date from which the economy can restart if the live records are lost. Segmented, manually operable fallbacks for power grids and water systems, so a compromised network does not mean a compromised plant. Paper and local-cache protocols in hospitals that allow care to continue for weeks, not hours, without central systems. Emergency payment and rationing mechanisms that can function when digital identity and banking rails go down. Regular, national-scale stress tests — not tabletop exercises for press releases — that simulate the simultaneous loss of finance, communications and logistics.

To be sure, America is not starting from zero. Banks keep backups. Utilities have cybersecurity teams. Agencies like the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), the federal government's lead civilian cyber-defense agency, exist for a reason. Critics will rightly say a total, permanent erasure of the financial system is unlikely, and that humans have muddled through blackouts and disasters before. They are half right. Backups that have never been restored at scale under attack are not a plan; they are an assumption. A patient intruder can also corrupt the backups months before pulling the trigger. And past blackouts were local, temporary and analog-supported in ways today's systems no longer are. We survived because the surrounding system survived. The risk AI raises is simultaneity: many systems, in many places, hit by actors who can adapt faster than our incident-response calls can convene.

We do not need to choose between innovation and preparation. We need to stop pretending prevention alone is preparation. Keep competing. Keep building. Keep working on alignment and defense. But fund resilience as if failure is possible — because every serious engineer knows it is.

Civilization has given us an extraordinary margin for error. The poorest among us live atop miracles previous generations could not imagine. That margin is not natural. It is built, maintained and — if we are careless — losable in a cascade we have never rehearsed. The question is no longer just how fast AI should go. It is whether, when something critical breaks, we intend to have anything left to fall back on.
