GPT 6.2 Astra Solves a Historical Mystery
OpenAI’s newest model can autonomously research open historical problems from end to end, forming hypotheses to make new discoveries.

There’s a growing sense in the AI community that we’re approaching an intelligence explosion far faster than even the most optimistic accelerationists predicted. One of the most publicly visible signals pointing at that acceleration is the rapid progress made in mathematics in recent months.
In four years, LLMs have gone from scoring a D+ on elementary school math tests to literally solving hundreds of the most difficult problems in the field in the space of only a few weeks—decades of progress compressed into two months. Navier-Stokes, one of the central Millennium Prize Problems posed by the Clay Mathematics Institute more than a quarter century ago, fell in September. Last Friday, Anthropic researcher and mathematician Levent Alpöge announced a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, which paradoxically can be verified using a mathematics program called Lean, but has yet to be understood by human experts. Just last night, Anthropic casually posted a solution to the VP vs VNP problem without any fanfare. Such things are becoming the norm.
Because mathematics is deterministic, though, observers have wondered whether this same process of research and discovery will generalize to non-deterministic domains like history. In this blog post, I will present evidence that this is indeed starting to happen.
I recently had the opportunity to test OpenAI’s new model, GPT-6.2 Astra, before it was released and it made a significant historical discovery. This required it to read through sources, develop a hypothesis with limited evidence, and make an argument. Moreover, it would be unlikely that a human could replicate what it did.
With only minimal prompting, GPT-6.2 Astra was able to autonomously identify the creator of an anonymous 1808 sketchbook held at Yale’s Beinecke Library. It did so extremely quickly (in just 22 minutes), using publicly available primary and secondary sources. To do so, it discovered a little known source in a paper archive, predicted that it would prove its hypothesis if examined and, in the end, it did. Not only that, but this new source turns out to be a major find on its own that will be of significant interest to both Canadian and fur trade historians. While it’s a narrow example, it’s representative of the historical research process as a whole and a sign of things to come.

Testing A New Model
I started testing GPT-6.2 Astra in late October in the ChatGPT Windows Desktop app. It is a quirky but highly capable model, especially well suited to long-running, open-ended tasks that might take hours or days to complete. I’ve found that its results were remarkably reliable and consistent, allowing me to contemplate tasks that were too difficult or long-running for previous models. For example, I had it examine and correct an 8,002-row CSV transcription of the Shipboy & Henry daybook. Last fall, I was amazed that Gemini 3 Pro could work in pounds, shillings, and pence. GPT-6.2 used the internal math of the ledger to verify and fix transcriptions against 353 images. In essence, it tested the handwritten math across thousands of quantities, item prices, sub-totals and account totals over the course of only a few hours and made edits accordingly. I had previously tried this with both GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5 and neither one completed the task as thoroughly or competently.
Even so, GPT-6.2 Astra sometimes communicates in a weird way, writing backwards-speak, riddle-like answers that seem wrong at first glance but turn out to be technically correct. It often feels like I’m reading a formal legal disclaimer, but one written in a friendly, colloquial way. The strangest thing, though, is that the underlying work product is always sound.
My intuition suggests this is a product of a post-training regime aimed at optimizing the model towards deterministic tasks like creating mathematical proofs. My sense is that GPT-6.2 Astra’s prose gets weird when it must communicate a final result based on incomplete evidence, or when there is no way of completing a task but it must still provide an answer. In math this would be a warning sign, but in non-deterministic domains it’s often the norm.The Anonymous Sketchbook
Despite the communication issues, the more I used GPT-6.2, the more it felt meaningfully different to me. It felt smarter, but I couldn’t really get a sense of how smart it actually was or where its limits were compared to other models like Opus 5.5 or Fable 5.2. So I decided to give it what I thought would be an impossible task, to see how it fared. I asked it to identify the creator of an anonymous sketchbook that has intrigued me for years.
In 2022, when I was working on the authorship of Alexander Henry’s Travels and Adventures in Canada and the Indian Territories, I was going through the catalog of Yale University’s Beinecke Library when I came across a reference to an anonymous sketchbook depicting an 1808 journey from Montreal to Fort William and back. The sketchbook had apparently been in private hands for decades until Yale bought it from an antiquarian bookseller in 2020. It’s not a particularly well-known document, but it’s important because it uniquely depicts a number of locations in the Great Lakes that were destroyed only a few years later. I’ve spent many hours over the years trying to figure out who made the sketches with no luck.
The sketchbook, which is browsable on the Beinecke Library site, has a mottled, leather cover and contains 84 pages, 34 of which feature pencil and ink drawings or watercolours; most are labelled, only three are dated. The handwriting is limited to short captions but is highly distinctive, with capital letters that extend forward under the line and long-tailed d’s that curl back to embrace the entire word.
“Fort at St. Joseph’s,” from the anonymous 1808 sketchbook, fol. 17r. Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Yale University, WA MSS S-4271.
The unknown artist began the book in March 1808 and it opens with a depiction of the Newfoundland shoreline as seen from a passing ship. This is followed by pictures of the people and landscapes of Lower Canada (now the Province of Quebec) including Sir John Johnson’s house southeast of Montreal, the fortifications at Chambly, and a few churches. It continues with renderings of Chaudière Falls at modern-day Ottawa (dated 8th June) and the entrance of the Rivière Creuse (11 June). Following these are drawings, amongst others, of Fort St. Joseph on Lake Huron, Fort William on Lake Superior, islands in Lake Erie, and the mountains at Lake Champlain as drawn from the St. Lawrence, dated to 16 August.
The chief problem is that while the book is unique, the route of travel it records was common. Every year, hundreds of canoes and bateaux made the same journey. The artist could literally be any one of several thousand people. Moreover, only a tiny fraction of the written sources that once existed survive today, and those that do are handwritten, often un-indexed, and mostly un-transcribed, which makes search efforts inefficient in the extreme—especially because they also tend to be scattered in unlikely places across dozens of archives. Without knowing the person’s name in advance, it is almost impossible to know where to start looking, which is why the document has remained anonymous for more than 200 years.Waiting For Chabot
One morning, early on in my testing, I gave GPT-6.2 Astra access to the images of the sketchbook and, in retrospect, a throwaway prompt:
“identify the artist who painted these images…For context, they are in the Yale Beinecke Rare Book Room. I found them when researching an article I wrote on the authorship of Alexander Henry's Travels and Adventures. I have wondered if they might be connected to with Kendall or Berczy but have not pursued either lead. Determine who likely painted these images.”
I included those last lines as I’d always wondered if it was related to Alexander Henry’s memoir or either of those two people but, as it turns out, my “assistance” only led the model on a brief wild goose chase. After pointing the model at the sketchbook’s images, I attached the article I’d written on Kendall and Henry for context and then set GPT-6.2 Astra to work on Ultra effort level in goal mode, which encourages it to work longer.
Turns out, I didn’t need goal mode. Only 22 minutes and 1 second later, it came back with a remarkably clear and decisive answer: “Louis-Guy-Charles-Guillaume de Rohan-Chabot, vicomte de Chabot (1780–1875), probably made the travel drawings.” Moreover, it suggested that we should test the hypothesis by ordering copies of a microfilmed diary of Chabot’s trip it had found in an archival inventory, which was held in the Special Collections of Cleveland State University. I was truly stunned. I’d never heard of Chabot, let alone suspected that he might have been responsible for the drawings. I certainly thought it improbable that he might have also left an unknown journal about the Northwest, given how well trodden the field is!
Who was this Chabot? A quick bit of research revealed that he’d been a minor French aristocrat and Irish landowner whose family was displaced by the French Revolution. In exile, he became a British Army officer and spent most of 1808 in Canada where he was posted as Deputy Adjutant General.1 Eventually his daughter married the Marquis de Lafayette’s grandson and his papers were preserved in the family archive, lost until they were discovered by a descendant in 1984.
I cannot emphasize enough how obscure and almost totally unknown Chabot is to North American and Canadian historians. The only scholarly reference I could find to his 1808 trip to Canada was in fur trader George Nelson’s journals, published by Harry Duckworth as Friends, Foes, and Furs in 2019. In his journal, Nelson mentioned hearing that a “Count de Chabot” had visited Fort William and so, as editor of the text, Duckworth provided a paragraph of context for readers. Dr. Duckworth has an encyclopedic knowledge of fur trade sources, but neither he nor any of the other experts I’ve shared this finding with have given Chabot much thought, if they’ve even heard of him. No one would have thought to connect him to the sketchbook or suspected that he might have left a written account of his journey. But why would we?
George Nelson’s journal, 1 August 1808: “A Great Personage is said to have come to Fort William by the name of Count de Chabot.” Toronto Public Library, Baldwin Collection of Canadiana, George Nelson Papers, S 13, Journal, 3 November 1807–31 August 1808.
Initially, I felt the response was too improbable to be correct and I suspected that either the journal would prove to be something else entirely (perhaps miscataloged) or at best that it would disprove the result. But order the journal I did. It turns out, there are actually two parts to it: one covering Chabot’s travels in Lower Canada and the other his journey to the Upper Country. The first is held in a private, family archive in France. The second was microfilmed by the Library of Congress as part of its Lafayette papers and was available to order from Cleveland State University. How GPT-6.2 Astra actually learned about it remains a bit unclear, but it traced its finding to a single footnote in a 2014 PhD thesis on aristocratic refugee families fleeing the French Revolution, written by historian Juliette Reboul.2 Again, not somewhere a human historian of the fur trade would tend to look.
After some back and forth with both archives, all three documents arrived in my inbox. The first thing I saw was the handwriting, which featured the same distinctive letter forms as the sketchbook. When I read through the Upper Country diary from CSU, its itinerary aligned perfectly with the dated drawings, placing the diarist in the same places on the same days. But then I found the type of conclusive evidence we rarely get as historians: on 18 June 1808, after Chabot’s canoe reached Fort St. Joseph, he wrote “I took a sketch” before describing the exact view of the fort depicted in the matching drawing. It was unreal: X never marks the spot.
Chabot’s entry for 18 June 1808, pp. 36–37 of his “Journal to the Upper Country”: on landing at St. Joseph’s he “went over the Fort,” of which “I took a sketch.” Lafayette Microfilm Collection, reel 55, Special Collections, Cleveland State University.
But that is just the first part of the find. The journal itself is long and detailed, covering some 162 notebook pages that provide numerous new insights into fur trade life. Interestingly, Chabot made an extended stop at and study of Niagara Falls, although no images of it survive in the sketchbook, which raises the possibility that other drawings or watercolours may survive elsewhere. Either way, lengthy new journals like this are simply never discovered these days.
Attached to the journal was another trove of documents too: excerpts from long-destroyed North West Company papers, journals, and letters that shed new light on the business and social structure of the fur trade, Indigenous ethnography, and the origins of one of the most historic and important document collections in Canada, the Masson Collection. Again, this almost never happens.
What a find! And in 22 minutes.
ShareA RoboHistorian and its Sources
How did GPT-6.2 Astra reach its conclusion so quickly? We can’t know for sure because the models are black boxes, but its sequence of reasoning trace summaries and tool calls suggests that it approached the problem very much as a human historian might—but that it could also harness capabilities that most of us simply do not have.
It began by reading my article (which turned out to be misleading but qualifies here as the only relevant secondary literature) and then went through the images from the sketchbook one by one. It noted the dated images specifically, indicating they could be used to test potential candidates against other documents. It was on that basis that it quickly eliminated both the men I had suggested in my prompt by placing them elsewhere when specific drawings were made. Then it started reading through all the primary documents it could find from the same archival sites that a historian would consult if they too were working on the problem.
But here is where Astra did something unusual, which appears humanlike at first glance although it most certainly is not. Its critical find was the diary of Joseph Frobisher, a retired fur trader who kept a daily journal of his social engagements in Montreal. I’d cited the diary in my Kendall article (without mentioning Chabot or the sketchbook), but the model accessed the original on its own. As it recorded in its reasoning traces:
“I found a promising entry in Frobisher’s diary: on June 6, 1808, the vicomte de Chabot left Lachine for Kaministiquia with William McGillivray and William McKay. Five days later, the sketchbook places its traveller on the Ottawa River. Chabot also kept a journal of that journey, which gives us a way to test him against his companions.”
It then traced Chabot west through other papers, placing him at Fort St. Joseph later that month via a letter written by a man named John Askin Jr. that had been published in a document collection nearly a hundred years ago.Why Human Historians Didn’t Make the Connection
Theoretically, this might sound doable for a human historian too, but think about how one would actually need to make these connections in practice. First of all, most human historians starting from the sketchbook alone wouldn’t think to look in Joseph Frobisher’s Montreal dinner diary. Why would they? I certainly hadn’t. It had nothing to do with the sketchbook. Only in hindsight, once you know about Chabot and who he was, would it be reasonable to conclude that he might have dined with Frobisher in Montreal.
Let’s assume for a second, though, that a historian with better intuition had decided to check Frobisher’s book anyway. Remember: they don’t know who they’re looking for, just that the sketchbook’s creator was in Montreal in the spring of 1808 and made the journey westwards in early June. In that case, Chabot’s name is still unlikely to stand out as the diary is filled with the names of dozens of fur traders, almost all of whom were going to Fort William by canoe in spring. To single out Chabot for special consideration, upon seeing his name you’d also need to simultaneously recall that in an obscure, century-old book of letters, John Askin Jr. (an even more obscure man than the Count) met Chabot at Fort St. Joseph in mid-June later that year. Even then, I’m not sure that would be enough. No one knew Chabot was an artist.
Although we can construct a coherent narrative in hindsight, I cannot emphasize enough how exceedingly difficult this sort of thinking and recall is for humans. I know I could not have done it. I’ve also spoken to several of the most capable and experienced researchers in fur trade history who were equally surprised by these findings. Unless one wanted to make identifying the author of the sketchbook their life’s work, it is exceedingly improbable that anyone would have identified Chabot as its creator, without a healthy dose of serendipity.
Unlike a human, Astra was able to tackle the problem from both ends at once: working forward from the scant facts of the 1808 journey pictured in the sketchbook and backwards from the immense number of surviving facts about 1808 British North America in general.
I should also point out here that these abilities are not entirely unique to GPT-6.2 Astra. I subsequently ran the same test (using the same prompt) with Fable 5.2 and it reached the same conclusion in about 35 minutes. Not surprisingly, it provided a more coherent and articulate explanation for its findings, but its reported reasoning/research path was almost identical to Astra’s. Nevertheless, it fell a bit short of Astra’s overall accomplishment: Fable failed to identify Chabot’s journal, which turned out to be the most important piece of evidence for the sketchbook and a significant find on its own. What this suggests, though, is that these capabilities are starting to exist generally at some level across the frontier models.Conclusion
The identification of Count Chabot as the creator of the Yale sketchbook and the recovery of his largely unknown diary will be of real interest to historians of both Canada and the fur trade. That an AI model was able to autonomously make this discovery and contribute new knowledge to these fields, is remarkable.
Nevertheless, GPT-6.2 Astra still had a strange tendency to shy away from its own accomplishments, which also reveals the limitations of current systems on non-deterministic tasks. When given a copy of the diary, which proved the case beyond any shadow of a reasonable doubt, Astra started to hedge as it had done at the end of other long tasks: it worried that because the diary was not signed, it might not be Chabot’s or that because not every sketch was mentioned in the diary, perhaps not all of them were his. Both improbable in the extreme. It also preferred to describe the diary as “an anonymous diary attributed to Chabot” rather than say it was written by him.
While self-criticism is important, excessive or unreasonable doubt is counterproductive for a researcher. I would attribute Astra’s behaviour to the same math-focused post-training regime I speculated about earlier. For now I suspect it will limit the model’s ability to scaffold research problems one onto the other. This too will almost certainly pass, though, and probably soon given how rapidly these capabilities are evolving.
To my mind, this puts historians in a similar position as the mathematicians found themselves about eleven months ago, when LLMs started to solve the first Erdős problems. Over the past year, we’ve watched these same models go from learning to read handwriting, to beginning to think historically, to mastering the undergraduate essay, and finally to making small but real contributions to human knowledge more or less autonomously. The trajectory is clear.
As always, these developments also seem to be outpacing our capacity to process and absorb their implications. But if the experience of mathematics is any guide, we can also anticipate that sometime next spring or summer we’ll face a sort of crisis of confidence as we struggle to understand how to respond to agentic systems that can do things human historians cannot. To be fair, that crisis will almost certainly be less profound for us than it is for math. The problem in mathematics, of course, is that solving hard math problems was the whole game. Historians, on the other hand, constantly reinterpret the past in light of an evolving present; we don’t chase a single universal truth. We also (in the ideal at least) write books and articles that are closer to works of philosophy and literature than they are to scientific tracts. Humanity is thus something of a requirement for the production of historical literature.
As I’ve argued before, history’s humanistic heritage may well insulate us, at least somewhat, from the coming effects of mechanized intelligence. But if that is to be the case, we desperately need to think about how we’ll co-exist with these AI historians. For that future is coming.3

But this isn't the work of historians. It's not an interpretation. It's a very narrow kind of problem that historians, archivists and literary scholars occasionally have to work on--establishing provenance, authorship, etc. on particular documents and manuscripts held within archives, and using a form of forensic analysis to establish possible connections between texts about which we know less than we might like. It's important work, and it's not unprecedented to be using computational tools in doing it--there were scholars using topic modelling and network analysis to approximate some of this kind of analysis. But the end it's only nailing down one kind of precondition for historical analysis. The documents that get identified or reclassified through this work do not speak for themselves or contain self-evident meaning or significance. That's up to us still. That's what matters. And that kind of interpretation is not like a mathematical proof.
