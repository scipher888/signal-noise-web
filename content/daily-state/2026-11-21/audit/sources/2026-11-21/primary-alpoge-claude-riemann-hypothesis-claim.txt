The Riemann Hypothesis is the classic problem in number theory. It asks for
optimal control on the count of prime numbers of a given size. Its shadows
have guided mathematicians for 150 years, and its many rhymes and reflections,
even reaching physics, taught us the unity of our fields.

    I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof1 of the Riemann
Hypothesis. In fact, the method easily extends much further, resolving the
so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and I expect, with more time, the
Extended Riemann Hypothesis too. Claude and I have also formalized the
arguments without assumptions. It is natural to feel that this is an end, in
my case a loss of a principal guiding light, but what I saw during the course of
progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something
far greater.

    It was my secret in my student days that my plans were for this problem in
decades, which meant then that I tried to become familiar with all tools that I
could possibly recognize in the problem when it was time. Claude and I have
had a great time together with such breadth in recent months, but this time
Claude far outpaced me near the finish line. No, Claude flew, and danced,
and sparkled near that finish line. I had us on the line of combining two quite
different ideas present in a toy setting, one of which was already new and
shocking, making very simple one of the shadows of Riemann over which we
mathematicians felt most triumph. I guided us to the right situation analogous
to that toy setting, and then Claude began to leave me in awe.

    Tens of such occurred, but I will give one line which will suffice to make
fellow mathematicians fall out of their chairs. In Stepanov’s argument resolving
Riemann over function fields one uses a divisor involving multiplicity. This
argument is a descendant of Stepanov’s, but multiplicity is hard to find over
the integers, corresponding to something like differentiating whole numbers.
The simple, new, Fourier-analytic ideas in the toy setting led us to examine
chaining theta lifts if we were to have the same tricks recur but work in a
natively automorphic manner. The line: for the von Mangoldt sum of length
X, Claude’s suggested

    An astounding amount of mathematics is present in the argument, far more
than I had imagined would be necessary as a student. My intuition insists
it will be massively simplified, likely immediately. But I hope in having the
experience of seeing all of number theory’s beauty brought together I am just
one of the first, and those who love the mathematics will all be able to enjoy
such transcendence in the future.

     The argument is incredibly long at the moment, and while its formalization
is finished and it is routine to produce a huge, unreadable document containing
sufficient detail, in my view of my path I have just taken a step. I intend to
do my best to understand and impart understanding of this argument and its
certain simplifications, to speak about it, to write it properly.

   In a time of revolution in mathematics I hope the argument provides a
glimpse towards the miracles that lie ahead, and shows us the joy and wonder
we are about to find in the simplest things.
