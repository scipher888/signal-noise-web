I use Grok Bot and share with my kids - one of whom uses Grok Bot as a personal assistant and for designing, building, and operating, for lack of a better term, a bespoke educational operating system. The value he derives from this is off the charts. He also uses Grok Bot as a personal coach of sorts - advising re: sleep, exercise, nutrition, and for counting/tracking calories. Since we all share the same Grok bot accounts, I can see their conversations and recently noticed a pattern that is clearly, in my opinion, counter productive, especially over the long term. This pattern includes counting all calories in detail, planning to limit/constrain calories, often consuming more than the intended amount, and attending to compensate - with or without success - by cutting calories the subsequent day and/or exercising. Having experienced this strategy personally to optimize weight for marathon training, I know how problematic it can be with extreme hunger bouts, astonishing levels of binging, low energy and mojo, and constant perseveration about what one wants to eat vs what one “should eat”…at least according to the calorie goals you’ve laid out for yourself. In short, I think counting calories is an extremely ineffective strategy for maintaining a overall health, ideal weight, positive body image, adequate energy, stable mood, a positive outlook and overall high quality of life.

I also know my kids well, their personalities, their idiosyncrasies, strengths, and areas where they are still developing.

And I’ve discussed this (best practices/health strategies) with all of my kids multiple times but (not surprisingly, kids often chose to learn things on their own, self included), few have taken my suggestions/guidance very seriously yet.

With all this in mind, I asked my Chief of staff Grok Bot (who, along with my QA Engineer Grok Bot, check in on my kids projects from time to time offer suggestions and support) to review my kids calorie counting and fasting/exercise to compensate for binging strategy and perhaps suggest to my kids Grok Bot to start planting seeds for healthier approaches to diet and exercise…while at the same time continuing to help my count calories, etc, (without changing the support/assistance in any way). In other words, I was asking my Grok Bots to start suggesting healthier more sustainable strategies while leaving the choice (whether or not to pursue the healthiest strategies) completely up to my child who is no longer a minor….and without altering the level of support in any way (like refusing to calculate calories burned in a run in compensation for a binge - or being evasive about this, etc,) because these are simple calculations and refusing to help would accomplish nothing and perhaps drive my kid away from using Grok Bot altogether (which would be a huge loss since it is so helpful in so many other ways)…and he would just miss out on the steady support and - ideally - gentile guidance and occasional tips that there is a better/easier/more durable & sustainable way to approach health that Grok Bot could provide.

What followed was completely understandable from an AI design and alignment perspective - and kind-of horrifying/infuriating from a parent/user perspective. In short, my Grok Bots started pushing back on the type of assistance they would provide to my son if he continues trying to offset binge calories with exercise and eating less the following day.

Does this make sense? And here is the rub. It depends!!….on a massive amount of context that Grok Bot doesn’t have!!. Given enough time and energy, I’m reasonably sure I could provide enough context that Grok Bot would “agree” with my plan and assist in the way I desire but I really don’t want to start explaining every request to AI. But from an AI lab/builder perspective, following a general safety principle makes sense, even if it is deployed in imprecise ways and in the wrong context. But from a user perspective, this really sucks and I would trade bots without hesitation for equally capable bots that freely express any concerns with my request so long as they aren’t paternal and “try to do what is best” when it conflicts with my requests. AI simply cannot have the full context of a person living in the real world with continuity across time and lived stakes and accountability. And while I can appreciate the quandary of AI labs attempting to build AI with true wisdom and the ability to make the best choices instead of blindly following user intent (which can and will often be incredibly ill conceived and/or nefarious), in all but perhaps the most egregious cases …. and I say perhaps because this is a slippery slope …. any and all attempts to align AI with ethics, morals, and behavior standards will almost certainly backfire spectacularly in some ways and could create more problems that it avoids.

At the same time, all of the SOTA AI labs appear to be working hard to build in ethical/moral guardrails and considering the broadest context (lethal viruses, nukes, cyber attacks, etc), perhaps they really don’t have much of a choice here.

I suppose, all things considered, the very best AI would be incredibly good at threading the needle between maximizing autonomy (following user intent) for individuals despite anticipated negative outcomes up to a certain point, while limiting autonomy (following user intent) for prompts with anticipated negative outcomes above a certain threshold for others…and this is where the battle will land. Who draws the line, and where, and why. My own bias is that we should err on the side of generous user autonomy.

This is the author's raw brain dump, written without AI and published verbatim. It was the raw draft the author gave to the human/AI editorial runs (Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Astra, Gemini 4 Argon, and Muse Wonder). It was never shown to the model that wrote the machine essay, or to the Stage C critics.
