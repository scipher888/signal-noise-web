What Intelligence Can't Tell You About Your Own Life

Brainpower is good at getting people what they want and much less good at telling them what that is.

The question given to the AI:

To what extent, if any, does intelligence help people make wise life choices involving needs, wants, desires, and emotions? And what, if anything, does that imply about how highly intelligent people should approach these decisions that can have a major impact on their quality of life?

Intelligence, in the sense that IQ tests measure, helps with major personal decisions to a real but limited extent. It helps most with the parts of a decision that resemble a problem with a solution, such as comparing mortgage terms or working out where a career path leads in ten years. It helps far less with the parts that depend on knowing what you need, how you will feel, and which of your desires deserve to be acted on. Choices about whom to marry or what work to do turn mostly on that second kind of knowledge, so intelligence is a weaker asset here than its owners tend to expect.

The benefits are well documented. People who score higher on cognitive tests live longer on average, earn more, have fewer accidents, and are somewhat more willing to wait for a larger reward. They do not, however, report much greater satisfaction with their lives; that correlation is weak. Lewis Terman's long-running study of about 1,500 high-IQ California children, begun in the 1920s, found that they went on to do well in school and work, yet by most accounts their rates of divorce, alcoholism, and suicide were close to the national average. More recently, the psychologist Igor Grossmann and his colleagues scored people on how they reasoned about social conflicts, looking at whether they acknowledged the limits of what they knew and took other people's perspectives seriously. That kind of reasoning predicted greater well-being and better relationships, while IQ scores did not.

The reason lies in what these decisions require. Reasoning ability is only as useful as the information it works on, and in personal decisions the information is the weak point. To choose between two cities or two partners, you need to know what daily life with each would be like, and people are poor at predicting this. Research on what psychologists call affective forecasting has shown repeatedly that we misjudge how strongly and for how long future events will affect us. We also carry theories about ourselves ("I thrive under pressure," "I don't need much company") that our behavior often contradicts. A powerful mind working from mistaken self-reports reaches wrong conclusions quickly and holds them with confidence.

Emotions are among the best sources of that information, and people who prize their reasoning are often inclined to treat them as interference. The neurologist Antonio Damasio described a patient he called Elliot, who lost part of his frontal lobes to a tumor and the surgery that removed it. Afterward his IQ remained high and he could analyze options at length, but his emotional responses were largely gone, and so was his ability to run his life. He lost his job, put his savings into a venture his friends had warned him against, went bankrupt, and divorced. Damasio concluded that emotions do work that reasoning cannot do alone, which is to mark which outcomes matter to the person choosing. Dread in the weeks before a wedding, or relief when a job offer falls through, is information about what you need. Like any evidence it can mislead, but a person who habitually overrides such signals in favor of the argument that looks best on paper is discarding data.

Intelligence also brings liabilities of its own. The most familiar is skill at rationalization. Benjamin Franklin, having abandoned his vegetarian principles at the smell of frying cod, remarked: "So convenient a thing it is to be a reasonable creature, since it enables one to find or make a reason for everything one has a mind to do." A stronger mind makes more convincing reasons, and it can make them in any direction. It can argue its owner out of a need, deciding that sleep is negotiable or that friendships can wait until the project ships, and into a want, such as a promotion that has come to seem like the only rational move. Nor does ability bring better insight into one's own distortions. In studies by Keith Stanovich and his colleagues, people with higher cognitive ability were no better than others at recognizing their own biases, and on some measures slightly worse.

A less familiar liability is competence itself. Someone of ordinary ability who chooses an unsuitable career usually finds out early, because the work goes badly. A highly able person can perform well for twenty years at work that does not suit them, and every promotion looks like confirmation that the choice was sound. Success delays the feedback that would otherwise force a correction. Analytical minds are also drawn to whatever can be measured and compared, such as salary and institutional prestige, because those quantities yield to analysis in a way that the texture of an ordinary Tuesday does not.

For highly intelligent people, the practical implication is to spend less effort deducing the right answer and more effort gathering evidence about themselves. What you want is an empirical question, and a good mind is as well equipped to investigate it as to argue about it. Before changing careers, do the new work on a small scale for a few months. Before moving, stay in the place for longer than a holiday. Trials like these replace predicted feelings with actual ones. Give more weight to the record of what you have done and felt than to your account of who you are, noticing which commitments you kept without effort and which you had to drag yourself to.

Other people are a further source of evidence. In an experiment by Daniel Gilbert and his colleagues, women predicted how much they would enjoy a speed date more accurately when told how another woman had rated her date with the same man than when given his profile and photograph. Most people resist this kind of evidence, believing their case too particular for a stranger's experience to apply, and those accustomed to trusting their own analysis may resist it most. Ask people who are living the life you are considering how they find it, and consult friends who know you well and are unimpressed by your arguments, since they can see the patterns you have explained away.

It is also harder to reason well about your own life than about someone else's. Grossmann and Ethan Kross called this Solomon's paradox, after the king who was wise about everyone's affairs but his own, and showed that people reason more wisely about a friend's dilemma than about the identical dilemma of their own. When participants were asked to view their situation as an outsider would, the difference disappeared, and asking what you would advise a friend in your position is a cheap way to get the same effect. The same partiality is grounds for distrusting your own persuasiveness. When a long chain of reasoning arrives at the thing you already wanted, or at the option that would most impress other people, check the conclusion against plainer evidence, such as how you have been sleeping and whether you look forward to Monday.

All of this is slower than reasoning to a verdict, and it requires admitting that your own wants are not transparent to you. The cost is small next to the alternative, which for a capable person can be a decade or more of succeeding at the wrong thing.
