The Machinery of Being Wrong

Humanity's future depends less on any resource than on our ability to notice our mistakes and fix them.

Ask people what humanity most needs to protect, and the answers come quickly: clean water, fertile soil, a stable climate, peace between nuclear powers, the health of the oceans. Each is a serious answer. Without any one of them, life on Earth would become harsher, poorer, and shorter. But if we are looking for the single element most vital to our long-term quality of life, we should look one level deeper, beneath the resources themselves to whatever allows us to keep them. That deeper element is our collective capacity for error correction: the ability of a society to notice that something is going wrong, to say so out loud, and to change course before the damage becomes permanent.

This can sound abstract next to the concrete urgency of a drying aquifer or a warming sea. Consider, though, how every one of those concrete problems has been solved or worsened. In the 1970s, scientists Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland proposed that chlorofluorocarbons, then ubiquitous in refrigerators and spray cans, were destroying the ozone layer. Their claim was inconvenient to a large industry and initially contested. Yet within about fifteen years, through open research, public debate, and political pressure, the world had signed the Montreal Protocol, and the ozone layer is now on a path to recovery. The chemistry mattered, but the decisive factor was that a warning could be raised, tested, believed, and acted upon.

Contrast that with the Soviet Union's embrace of Lysenkoism, the pseudoscientific agricultural doctrine that the state elevated above genetics for roughly three decades. Scientists who objected lost their posts, and some lost their lives. In China, a similar dynamic during the Great Leap Forward meant that local officials reported fantastical harvests because reporting the truth was dangerous. Grain was requisitioned on the basis of numbers everyone knew were false, and tens of millions of people starved. These were not failures of soil or weather. They were failures of feedback. The information that could have saved lives existed. It simply could not travel to the people with the power to act on it.

The economist Amartya Sen made this point memorably when he observed that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a relatively free press. His argument was not that democracies are wise or that journalists are saints. It was that when bad news can be published and leaders can be removed, those in power have strong reasons to respond to bad news before it becomes catastrophe. The protection does not come from good intentions. It comes from the machinery.

This is why error correction deserves priority over any single resource. The threats we can name today are not the only threats we will face. A century ago, almost no one was worried about atmospheric carbon, antibiotic resistance, or the fragility of global supply chains. A century from now, the defining problems may be ones we cannot currently imagine. We cannot prepare a specific solution for an unknown problem, but we can preserve the general capacity that solves problems of every kind. A civilization that keeps its ability to detect and fix mistakes can recover from almost anything short of sudden annihilation. A civilization that loses it will eventually fail, however rich its endowment, because it will keep making the same mistake with growing confidence.

Error correction is not a single institution. It is an ecosystem: a press that can investigate without permission, scientists who can publish unwelcome findings, courts that can rule against the powerful, elections that can remove leaders, and, beneath all of these, ordinary citizens who are willing to hear that they were wrong. It is fragile in ways that are easy to overlook. It rarely collapses all at once. It erodes when journalists are harassed or when local newspapers disappear and no one replaces them. It erodes when research funding depends on politically convenient conclusions, when platforms reward outrage over accuracy, and when people sort themselves into communities where every belief is confirmed and no correction can penetrate. Information can be suppressed by censorship, but it can also be drowned in noise, and the second method is harder to see and harder to resist.

Some mistakes can be undone: a bad law can be repealed, a failed technology abandoned, a polluted river slowly cleaned. Others close the door behind us. A species driven to extinction does not return when we regret it, the Ogallala aquifer took thousands of years to fill and is being pumped far faster than it recharges, and a government that abolishes free elections and independent courts may never permit them again. So the capacity to correct course comes with a companion rule: be slow to make choices that cannot be reversed, and slower still to destroy the things that let us notice when we are wrong.

So what is the primary action we should take? It is this: we must defend the channels through which bad news reaches power, and we must defend them most firmly when the bad news is about our own side. That means protecting the freedom to dissent, the independence of those whose job is to verify facts, and the ability of evidence to overturn the preferences of the people in charge, whether those people are governments, corporations, or popular movements.

The final clause matters most, because almost everyone supports free inquiry in principle and abandons it in practice. We are happy for critics to expose our opponents' errors. We are far less happy when the same scrutiny turns toward a cause we love, a leader we admire, or a policy we championed. Every regime that destroyed its own feedback loops did so in the name of something its supporters believed was urgent and good. The Lysenkoists believed they were feeding the people. The temptation to silence inconvenient voices is strongest precisely when we are most certain we are right, which is exactly when we most need to be checked.

In practical terms, defending these channels means supporting independent journalism, especially the unglamorous local reporting that tells a town its water is contaminated or its officials are corrupt. It means insisting that scientific institutions remain free to follow evidence and that their findings be public. It means resisting laws and norms that punish people for honest dissent, and designing our information systems, including increasingly powerful AI tools, so that they make truth easier to find rather than easier to bury. On a personal level, it means cultivating a habit that is rarer than it should be: treating the discovery of our own mistakes as a gain rather than a humiliation.

Someone might object that this is a luxury compared with the hard physical emergencies in front of us. If the climate is destabilizing now, shouldn't that be the priority? But the two are not in competition. Our response to climate change succeeds or fails largely through the same machinery: whether accurate measurements are trusted, whether failed policies can be abandoned, whether communities harmed by both the problem and the solutions can be heard. Protecting error correction is not a distraction from the planet's physical crises. It is the precondition for handling any of them well.

Humanity's long-term quality of life will not be secured by getting everything right. No society has ever managed that, and none ever will. It will be secured by remaining the kind of civilization that can find out when it has gone wrong and has the freedom and humility to change. The resources of the Earth are precious, but the most precious thing we have is our ability to learn from losing them. We should guard that ability as though everything else depends on it, because it does.
